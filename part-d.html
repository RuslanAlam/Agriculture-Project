Policies and Agribusiness Firms – Part D

By Ruslan Alam · AGBM 170

1. Farm and food policies (the Farm Bill)

Unlike tomatoes, soybeans are named directly in federal farm policy. They are a "covered commodity", which means soybean farmers can get payments from two safety-net programs run by USDA's Farm Service Agency:

  • Price Loss Coverage (PLC): pays farmers when the national average soybean price falls below a set "reference price."
  • Agriculture Risk Coverage (ARC): pays when a county's soybean revenue (yield × price) falls below a guaranteed level.

In July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act raised the soybean reference price by 19%, from $8.40 to $10.00 per bushel, raised the ARC guarantee from 86% to 90%, and extended these programs and marketing loans through 2031. For the 2025 crop only, farmers get whichever of the ARC or PLC payments is higher (American Farm Bureau; Congressional Research Service; farmdoc daily).

This connects to the budget in Part C. With soybean prices around $10 and a full-cost break-even price near $11, many farms were projected to lose money, and the higher reference price is meant to soften that. Energy policy also matters: the EPA Renewable Fuel Standard creates demand for soybean oil as biodiesel, which is why about 47% of U.S. soybean oil went to fuel in 2022 (see Part A).

2. Marketing policy

Grades and standards

USDA sets official grades for soybeans, from U.S. No. 1 to U.S. No. 4. Grades are based mainly on damaged kernels, foreign material (dirt, stems, weed seeds), and splits (broken beans) (USDA-AMS Soybean Standards). For example, U.S. No. 1 soybeans can have no more than 2% damaged kernels and 1% foreign material. In 2023, USDA dropped "soybeans of other colors" as a grading factor (DTN). Grades matter because buyers pay less for lower-grade beans, and exporters have to meet the grade in their sales contract.

The soybean checkoff

Soybeans don't have a marketing order like Florida tomatoes. Instead they have a national checkoff program, which USDA oversees (USDA-AMS). Every time a farmer sells soybeans, 0.5% of the sale price is taken out. Half goes to the national United Soybean Board (77 volunteer farmer-leaders), and half goes to state soybean boards. The money pays for research, promotion, and finding new markets. It collected $124.9 million in 2024 (United Soybean Board). The carbon footprint study in Part A was paid for by this checkoff.

3. Trade and tariff policy

The 2018 trade war

In July 2018, the U.S. put tariffs on Chinese goods, and China hit back with a 25% tariff on U.S. soybeans (Choices magazine). The chart shows what happened: U.S. soybean exports to China fell from 28.2 million metric tons in 2017/18 to 13.4 million in 2018/19, a drop of more than half. China bought more from Brazil instead. To help farmers, USDA created the Market Facilitation Program (MFP). In 2018 it paid about $8.5 billion, and about 85% of that went to soybean farmers (farmdoc daily).

The 2025 trade war

It happened again in 2025. After new U.S. tariffs, China stopped buying U.S. soybeans for months. Before that, China had bought about half of U.S. soybean exports (KCUR/NPR; NPR). China started buying again in October 2025 after a trade agreement, but by December it had bought less than 3 million metric tons, well short of the 12 million tons it promised (CNBC).

Tariffs on imports

Since the U.S. grows far more soybeans than it needs (see Part B), import tariffs don't matter much for soybeans. The trade policy that matters is other countries' tariffs on U.S. soybeans.

4. Agribusiness firms

List of companies

A few very large companies handle most of the world's soybeans once they leave the farm. They are known as the "ABCD" grain traders (Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy):

  • ADM (Archer Daniels Midland): buys, crushes, and ships soybeans
  • Bunge: oilseed processing and trading
  • Cargill: the largest private company in the U.S.; grain trading and processing
  • Louis Dreyfus Company: grain and oilseed trading

Bunge, Cargill, and ADM together were found to crush about 71% of the U.S. soybean crop (Oxfam, 2012; this is an older and activist source, so the exact number may have changed). On the seed side, Bayer (which bought Monsanto, the maker of Roundup Ready soybeans) and Corteva sell most of the GMO seed that farmers plant.

Company profile: ADM

ADM (Archer Daniels Midland) started in 1902 as a small linseed-crushing business in Minneapolis, Minnesota. Today it is headquartered in Chicago, Illinois, with major operations in Decatur, Illinois, in the middle of soybean country. ADM buys crops from farmers, crushes soybeans into meal and oil, and turns them into food ingredients, animal feed, and biofuel. It runs more than 270 processing plants and 420 crop buying locations around the world. In 2024 it had about $85.5 billion in revenue and about 44,000 employees (Fortune; ADM). ADM is a good example of the "middle" of the soybean supply chain. Farmers sell to it, and food, feed, and fuel companies buy from it.

5. What are the overall implications for society of commercial soybean production?

Soybeans are a big success story in some ways. They give the world a cheap source of protein for animal feed and cheap cooking oil, they help U.S. exports, and they make their own nitrogen, so they need less fertilizer than corn. Raw soybeans also have a low carbon footprint (Part A).

But there are real costs too. Growing so much of one crop depends on heavy herbicide use, which led to weed resistance and dicamba drift damage. The global demand for soybeans has pushed farming into the Amazon and the Cerrado in South America. U.S. farmers have become dependent on one big customer, China, and on government payments when trade wars break out. A few large companies control most of the processing. And more and more soybean oil is going into fuel tanks instead of food, which ties the price of food to energy policy.

My overall opinion: soybeans are not "good" or "bad." They are a very efficient crop inside a system with some big risks: trade fights, environmental damage in other countries, and a lot of power in a few companies' hands. Most people never think about soybeans, but they are connected to what we eat, what we feed animals, and even what we put in our cars.
