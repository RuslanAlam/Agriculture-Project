Global Production, Imports and Exports – Part B

By Ruslan Alam · AGBM 170

Where are soybeans grown around the world, and why?

Soybeans are the biggest oilseed crop in the world. In 2024/25 they made up about 62% of all oilseeds grown, more than rapeseed (canola), sunflower seed, peanuts, and cottonseed put together (USDA-ERS Oil Crops Yearbook, Table 41). This page looks at who grows them, where U.S. soybeans are grown, and how much the U.S. trades.

1. Top ten soybean-producing countries

Source: UN Food and Agriculture Organization, FAOSTAT (crops and livestock products, soya beans, production). Million metric tons. The U.S. is shown in red.

The message of the two charts

The biggest change is at the top. In 2010 the United States was #1 with 90.7 million tons. By 2024, Brazil had more than doubled its crop (68.8 → 144.5 million tons, +110%) and passed the U.S. The U.S. still grew (+31%), and Argentina actually fell a little (−8%). Two newcomers joined the top 10: Russia (1.2 → 7.0 million tons) and a much bigger Ukraine (1.7 → 6.6). China grows only about 21 million tons, a small amount compared to how many soybeans it buys from other countries.

What the regional chart shows

World soybean production grew by 62% in 14 years, from 264 to 427 million metric tons. Almost all of that growth came from South America. Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, and Uruguay went from growing half of the world's soybeans to 55%. The U.S. also grew (+31%), but more slowly, so its share of the world crop fell from about one-third to 28%. The UN's FAOSTAT database shows the same trend in calendar years: world production grew from 265.1 million tons in 2010 to 397.7 million tons in 2024, a 50% increase (FAOSTAT). Brazil passed the U.S. as the world's #1 soybean grower in 2019/20 (International Journal of Plant Production).

Why South America?

  • New seeds for the tropics. Brazil's government research agency, Embrapa, bred a "tropical soybean" that grows well in hot weather and acidic soil. That opened up the huge Cerrado savanna in central Brazil to farming (OCL journal).
  • Lots of land. The state of Mato Grosso went from 2% of Brazil's soybeans in 1970 to over a quarter today (OCL journal). Some of this land came from clearing forest and savanna, which is the deforestation concern from Part A.
  • China's demand. China, where soybeans started, is now the world's biggest importer (National Geographic). It needs soybean meal to feed its pigs and chickens, and Brazil is its main supplier.

2. Total U.S. imports and exports, 1994–2024

The U.S. is a huge soybean exporter and a very small importer. Exports grew from 840 million bushels in 1994 to 1.88 billion in 2024, with a record of 2.27 billion bushels in 2020. In 2024, about 43% of the U.S. soybean crop was shipped overseas as whole beans, up from about a third in 1994. The line also shows how much exports depend on one buyer. The dip in 2018 and 2019 is the trade war with China (see Part D), and exports fell again in 2023.

Imports are tiny: only 29 million bushels in 2024, less than 1% of what the U.S. grows. The biggest jump, to 71.8 million bushels in 2013, came right after the 2012 drought cut the U.S. crop. Some of these are specialty beans, like organic soybeans (see question 4).

3. Top ten soybean-producing U.S. states

Two bar charts of production by the top ten U.S. states, one for the most recent year and one from about ten years earlier. Why are these states on the list, how did things change, and what is surprising?

U.S. soybeans are grown mostly in the Corn Belt and the Upper Midwest, usually in rotation with corn. Illinois was the top soybean state in 2022, followed by Iowa (Morning Ag Clips, using USDA-NASS data). Soybeans fit these states well: deep, rich soils, enough summer rain, and a short trip to river barges and crushing plants.

Source: USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, Quick Stats (soybeans, state level, final annual estimates). Million acres. I added Pennsylvania (in red) as an 11th bar, since that is where I go to school.

The top soybean states barely changed in 15 years. They are all in the Midwest and Great Plains. Illinois (10.2 million acres) passed Iowa to become #1 by 2025. The biggest mover was North Dakota, which jumped from #10 to #4 (4.1 → 6.5 million acres). The top 10 states together had about 63 of the 80 million soybean acres harvested in 2025 (almost 80%). Pennsylvania is small by comparison, with about 570,000 acres.

Note: these charts compare acres, which is how much land is used for soybeans. Acres track production closely, but they are not the same thing.

4. Where do U.S. soybean imports come from?

Two pie charts of the top sources of U.S. imports, one for 2024 and one for 2014, with a detailed explanation.

How I got the data

I used the USDA Foreign Agricultural Service's Global Agricultural Trade System (GATS), which publishes U.S. Census Bureau trade data. My steps:

  1. Opened Standard Query, kept Data Source on FAS U.S. Trade, and set Product Type to Imports – General.
  2. Set Product Groups to Harmonized (HS-10) so the search shows real product codes, then searched the text "Soybeans."
  3. The search returned several codes (screenshot below). The ones starting with 12010000 are old codes that were replaced in 2012, so they have no data for 2014–2024. 1208… codes are soy flour and meal, and 2008996100 is prepared soybeans, so those are not whole soybeans either. That left two current whole-soybean codes: 1201900010 (organic soybeans) and 1201900090 (soybeans, other).
  4. I chose 1201900090 – Soybeans, Other, the main code for regular whole soybeans. GATS only let me select one code at a time, so organic soybeans (1201900010) are not included in these charts.
  5. Partners: All Partners. Statistics: value (thousand $) and quantity (metric tons). Dates: annual, January–December, 2014 to 2024. Then Retrieve Data and exported the results to a CSV file.

What the charts tell us

In both years, U.S. soybean imports come almost entirely from two countries: Brazil and Canada. Together they supplied 82% of imports in 2014 and 98% in 2024. The biggest change is how much less the U.S. imported in 2024: about 265,000 metric tons, down 65% from 763,000 tons in 2014. That is very small next to the roughly 119 million tons the U.S. grew in 2024 (FAOSTAT), only about 0.2%.

Why Brazil? Brazil is the world's biggest soybean grower (question 1), but its sales to the U.S. jump around a lot. In my data, imports from Brazil were large in 2014, 2021, 2023, and 2024 and zero in 2015–2020 and 2022. 2014 matches a tight U.S. supply: U.S. soybean stocks at the end of the 2013/14 season fell to just 92 million bushels, the lowest in 30 years (USDA-ERS Oil Crops Yearbook, Table 3), so U.S. buyers most likely brought in Brazilian beans to fill the gap.

Why Canada? Canada is the steady supplier. It shipped about the same amount in 2014 and 2024 (125,000 vs. 119,000 tons), and since it is right next door, its soybeans can reach U.S. processors without crossing an ocean. Because Brazil's share shrank, Canada's share grew from 16% to almost 45%.

What surprised me: the U.S., the world's #2 soybean grower, still buys soybeans from other countries at all. The small amounts from China are also interesting. In 2024, China's soybeans cost about $1,740 per ton ($2.08 million for 1,192 tons) compared with about $410–$475 per ton from Brazil and Canada, so they are probably special food-grade beans, not ordinary soybeans for crushing.

China is still the #1 buyer, but it matters less than it did ten years ago. In 2014/15 China bought 59% of all U.S. soybean exports; by 2024/25 that was down to 44%, a drop of about 6.9 million metric tons. The total amount the U.S. exported barely changed (50.1 vs. 51.2 million tons), which means other buyers filled almost the whole gap. Mexico grew from 7% to 10%, Egypt went from about 1% to 6.5%, and Germany more than doubled its purchases.

My analysis: this is what the soybean industry calls "diversifying." After China cut back during the 2018 trade war (see Part D), U.S. exporters looked for other customers. Mexico is a natural fit because it is right next door. Even so, almost half of U.S. soybean exports still go to one country, so a fight with China can still hit soybean farmers hard, which is exactly what happened again in 2025.
