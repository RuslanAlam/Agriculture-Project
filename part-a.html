Soybeans – Preliminary Questions (Part A)

By Ruslan Alam · AG BM 170

Chosen crop: Soybeans (Glycine max)

I picked soybeans because they are one of the biggest crops in the United States, but most people almost never see a soybean. Very few are eaten whole. Most are crushed into soybean meal, which feeds chickens, pigs, and cattle, and soybean oil, which ends up in cooking oil, salad dressing, mayonnaise, and a long list of packaged foods. As one historian put it, soybeans took over the American diet "without anyone noticing". This page answers the six Part A questions.

1. Availability: per-person availability of soybeans, 1992–2022

Graph the yearly "per-person availability" of your crop from 1992 to 2022, then describe and explain the graph, including how USDA calculates it.

How USDA calculates "per-person availability"

The USDA Economic Research Service (ERS) runs the Food Availability (Per Capita) Data System. For each food, ERS starts with the total supply for the year and takes away everything that does not go to food in the U.S. What is left is divided by the U.S. population (ERS documentation):

The formula

Supply = beginning stocks + production + imports

Available for food = supply − exports − animal feed − seed − industrial uses (like biodiesel) − ending stocks

Per-person availability = available for food ÷ U.S. population

This number is a measure of how much food is available, not how much people actually eat. Some of it spoils or gets thrown away in stores, restaurants, and homes.

Why soybeans are a special case

Whole soybeans are not listed as a food in the ERS system, because almost all of them are crushed into meal and oil (see the diagram above). The way most Americans "eat" soybeans is as soybean oil. ERS used to report soybean oil under added fats and oils, but that series stopped after 2010, when the Census Bureau stopped the survey it was based on (ERS documentation). To cover 1992–2022, I used the same formula with the supply-and-use tables in the ERS Oil Crops Yearbook.

50.7 lbper person in 1992

What the graph shows

The green line is the soybean oil available to each American for food and other uses. It went up in the 1990s, from about 50.7 pounds per person in 1992 to a peak of 59.0 pounds in 2002. After that it went down for about ten years and has stayed flat at around 42–45 pounds since 2010, ending at 42.3 pounds in 2022. That is still a lot. It is close to a pound of soybean oil per person every week.

The gold line shows where the "missing" soybean oil went. Almost none was used for fuel before 2004, but by 2022 the U.S. was using 37.5 pounds per person of soybean oil to make biodiesel and renewable diesel. In 2022, almost half of all soybean oil used in the U.S. went into fuel tanks instead of food.

Why the lines moved

  • The drop after 2002 (trans fats): much of the soybean oil in processed food was "partially hydrogenated," which creates trans fats. The FDA required trans fat on nutrition labels starting in 2006, and later ruled that partially hydrogenated oils are no longer "generally recognized as safe" (FDA – Trans Fat). Food companies switched some of their recipes to other oils like canola and palm oil, so less soybean oil went into food.
  • The rise of biofuel: federal rules require fuel companies to blend biofuels into gasoline and diesel (EPA Renewable Fuel Standard), and soybean oil is one of the main things biodiesel is made from. Every year since 2013, more than 15 pounds per person went to fuel.

My takeaway: Americans are not eating less soybean oil because soybeans are disappearing. U.S. soybean oil production nearly doubled over these 30 years. It is because the use of the oil changed from mostly food to food plus fuel.

2. U.S. Production: soybeans, 1992–2022

Graph the yearly U.S. production of your crop from 1992 to 2022, then describe and explain the graph.

2.19 → 4.27billion bushels, 1992 → 2022
+95%growth in 30 years
4.46billion bushels, record year (2021)
≈116million metric tons in 2022

What the graph shows

U.S. soybean production almost doubled in 30 years, from about 2.19 billion bushels in 1992 to about 4.27 billion bushels in 2022. The line climbs in steps, but it is not smooth. The big drops happen in bad-weather years or bad-market years:

  • 1993 (1.87 billion): the lowest point on the graph, the year of the Great Midwest Flood.
  • 2003 and 2012 (2.45 and 3.04 billion): dry, hot summers cut yields. 2012 was a major drought year across the Corn Belt.
  • 2019 (3.55 billion): a very wet spring delayed planting, and farmers planted fewer soybeans during the trade war with China, which had been the biggest buyer of U.S. soybeans (ABC News).
  • 2021 (4.46 billion): the record year in this period.

Why production went up

Two things drove the growth. First, higher yields: the average U.S. soybean field went from 37.6 bushels per acre in 1992 to 49.6 in 2022, about 32% more beans from the same land (NASS yield chart). Second, more acres: harvested soybean acres grew from roughly 58 million to roughly 86 million, because farmers could sell more. Demand came from exports to China, from the animal-feed market as meat production grew, and later from biodiesel made from soybean oil. Better seed also helped. Today about 96% of U.S. soybean acres are planted with herbicide-tolerant (genetically engineered) seed (USDA-ERS).

3. Supermarket presence

Find your crop in a local supermarket, take photos, and look for the country-of-origin label.

You will almost never find plain dry soybeans in a regular grocery store, so I looked for soybeans in the forms people actually buy: frozen edamame (green soybeans), tofu, soy milk, and vegetable oil (which is usually 100% soybean oil). Under USDA's Country of Origin Labeling (COOL) rules, fresh and frozen vegetables must show where they were grown, so the frozen edamame bag is the key label. Processed products like tofu, soy milk, and oil do not have to follow COOL (USDA-AMS FAQ), but many brands still print where they are made.

4. Detailed written story

Find and describe the longest, most detailed, and most interesting written story or essay on your crop.

I started with a Google search for "soybean magazine article" and then ran the same search with "Atlantic," "New Yorker," and "New York Times Magazine" added. The magazine searches mostly turned up short news pieces about the trade war, not long features on soybeans. The best long stories I found are below.

  • Runner-up "How soybeans took over the American diet – without anyone noticing", Edward D. Melillo (environmental historian, Amherst College), The Conversation, Sept. 2026. It was republished by the Washington Post and others.

    npr.org/2025/10/09/…/american-soybean-farms

Why the National Geographic story is the best

The National Geographic article follows the soybean across its whole history, starting in China, where people were using soybeans as early as 9,000 years ago, and ending with today's giant global trade. It explains how a plant that was once a small side crop is now in everything "from candy to crayons," and it says that more than 60% of processed foods contain some kind of soy product. The story also shows how the map has flipped: China, where soybeans started, is now the world's biggest importer, and Brazil is the biggest exporter. A big reason is that the world is eating more meat, and more meat means more animal feed made from soybean meal. The article also covers the downside. Almost all U.S. soybeans are genetically modified, and in South America soybean fields have replaced parts of the Amazon rainforest. I picked this story because it connects many of the topics in this class (trade, livestock, technology, and the environment) through one crop, and it is written for regular readers, not experts.

5. Consumer, health, and social concerns

Construct an annotated list of consumer, health, and social concerns about your crop.

I searched for "consumer concerns soybeans," "health concerns soy," and "social concerns soybeans" and grouped similar results together. The concerns fall into two groups. Health concerns are about what happens when people eat soy. Social and environmental concerns are about how soybeans are grown and traded. Some worries, like soy and cancer, are mostly myths according to the best sources. Others, like allergies and deforestation, are real and well documented.

My takeaway: the health worries people talk about most online (hormones and cancer) are the weakest ones when you check reliable sources. The social and environmental issues (weed-killer drift, deforestation, and farmers depending on one big buyer) are less talked about but have stronger evidence behind them.

6. Carbon footprint

Find the carbon footprint of your crop in pounds of CO2-equivalent per pound, say whether it is low, medium, or high, and judge how believable the source is.

≈ 0.8 lbCO₂e per lb of raw soybeans at the farm
≈ 3.2 lbCO₂e per lb of tofu
≈ 99.5 lbCO₂e per lb of beef, for comparison

Answer: Raw soybeans grown in the Americas have a carbon footprint of about 0.8 pounds of CO2-equivalent per pound of soybeans at the farm gate (CarbonCloud). Since the unit is "per pound," it is the same number in kilograms (0.8 kg CO2e per kg). Soy foods have a higher number because of processing: tofu is about 3.2, soy milk about 1.0, and soybean oil about 6.3, since it takes a lot of beans to make a pound of oil (Our World in Data, from Poore & Nemecek 2018).

Low, medium, or high?

Low. At about 0.8 lb per lb, raw soybeans are near the bottom of the list, below wheat and rice. Even tofu (3.2) is about 30 times lower than beef and about 3 times lower than chicken. The main exception is soybean oil, which lands in the "medium" range with other cooking oils. One big factor is where the soybeans are grown. When soy is planted on land that was just cleared from rainforest, its footprint can jump far higher. A recent review of 27 studies found estimates for soybeans ranging from −11.4 to 22.0 kg CO2e per kg, mostly because of land-use change (Agricultural Systems systematic review). U.S. soybeans are grown on long-established farmland, so they sit on the low end.

How believable are these sources?

  • CarbonCloud (the 0.8 number) is a for-profit company that makes carbon footprint tools for food brands. That makes it a third-tier source, so I would not trust it alone. I used it because it is one of the few places that gives a simple farm-gate number for raw soybeans.
  • Poore & Nemecek (2018) is a top-tier source. It was published in the peer-reviewed journal Science and combines data from about 38,700 farms in 119 countries. Our World in Data, run from the University of Oxford, turned it into the easy-to-read chart I used.
  • The U.S. soy industry's own study (United Soybean Board and NOPA, 2024) looked at 454 U.S. farms and found the carbon footprint of U.S. soybeans dropped 19% between 2015 and 2021. It was peer reviewed, but it was paid for by the soybean industry, so I would call it second-tier and read it carefully.

Overall, I think the numbers are believable because very different sources (a company, a big university-backed study, and the industry itself) all put raw soybeans in the low category. The exact number moves around depending on the farm and the land, but the conclusion does not change.
